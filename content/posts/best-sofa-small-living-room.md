## The dimensions that matter (read before shopping)

Four numbers decide whether a sofa fits your small room:

1. **Width:** 170–200 cm for most small living rooms. Under 170 and two adults can't sit comfortably; over 200 and walkways die.
2. **Depth:** 85–95 cm overall. Over 95 cm the sofa eats the room; under 85 cm most adults can't sit back comfortably. Seat depth (cushion front to backrest) of 50–55 cm suits most bodies.
3. **Arm width:** under 15 cm per arm. Chunky 25 cm rolled arms steal 20 cm of seating for pure decoration — in a small room, that's a tax you can't afford.
4. **Back height:** under 85 cm. Low backs preserve sightlines across the room (critical in small spaces); high backs wall off the seating area visually.

**And the visual rule:** visible legs (10–15 cm clearance underneath) beat a skirt or block base every time. Seeing floor under the sofa tells the eye the room continues — it's the single biggest "looks smaller/looks bigger" lever in sofa choice.

## 1. The apartment sofa (best overall)

**Dimensions:** 170–190 cm wide, 85–90 cm deep, slim arms.

The apartment sofa is a standard three-seater scaled down about 15% — same proportions, smaller footprint. It's the best overall because it seats three (or two sprawled) without demanding the 220 cm wall a standard sofa needs. If your wall is 240–280 cm, this is your sofa.

**Why it wins:** real three-person seating in a small-room footprint, widely available, and it doesn't scream "small furniture" — it just looks like a sofa.

**Watch for:** "apartment" in the name doesn't guarantee small dimensions — always check the spec sheet. Some brands slap the label on a 205 cm sofa.

## 2. The loveseat (best for tiny rooms)

**Dimensions:** 140–165 cm wide, 85–90 cm deep.

For rooms under 3×3.5 m, or as the second piece alongside an armchair. A loveseat plus one good armchair often seats three more comfortably than a cramped three-seater — and the two-piece arrangement gives you layout flexibility a single sofa can't.

**Why it wins:** fits walls as short as 180 cm, leaves room for a side table and a walkway, and pairs beautifully with an accent chair.

**Watch for:** the "loveseat trap" — buying a loveseat for a room that could fit an apartment sofa, then wishing you had the extra seat every movie night. If the wall allows 190 cm, take the apartment sofa (#1).

## 3. The chaise-end sofa (best for loungers)

**Dimensions:** 170–190 cm wide, 85–90 cm deep on the sofa side, 140–160 cm deep on the chaise side.

One end extends into a chaise — a full stretch-out lounge spot without the footprint (or commitment) of a sectional. In a small room, it gives you the sectional's best feature (the lounge seat) at two-thirds the floor cost.

**Why it wins:** the chaise is the most-used seat in any house — it will be fought over. And unlike a sectional's corner, the chaise end doesn't block a walkway or window.

**Watch for:** chaise orientation (left vs right) is permanent on most models — tape out BOTH orientations in your room before ordering. A chaise blocking the balcony door is a daily annoyance. Reversible-chassis models exist and are worth the small premium.

## 4. The sleeper sofa (best for guests)

**Dimensions:** 170–200 cm wide as a sofa; needs 200–230 cm clear in FRONT to open.

The honest trade-off: sleepers are heavier, firmer, and more expensive than equivalent regular sofas — you're paying for the mechanism. But if guests sleep over more than ~6 times a year and you have no guest room, it's cheaper than a decade of hotels for your visitors (and kinder than an air mattress).

**Why it wins:** genuine guest bed without a guest room. Modern mechanisms (especially click-clack and trifold) are vastly better than the bar-in-your-back pullouts of the past.

**Watch for:** measure the OPEN footprint, not just the sofa — the bed extends 200+ cm into the room. Tape it out. And sit on it first if you can: some sleepers sacrifice daily comfort for the mechanism. Our [rug size guide](/blog/rug-size-small-living-room/) will help you plan the floor space around it.

## 5. The modular sofa (best for changers)

**Dimensions:** per-module, typically 70–90 cm square seats; configure 2–4 modules.

Individual seat modules you arrange and rearrange — loveseat today, chaise tomorrow, separate chairs when guests come. For renters and restless rearrangers, it's the only sofa that adapts when the room (or apartment) changes.

**Why it wins:** moves in pieces (solves the delivery problem below), reconfigures endlessly, and single damaged modules can be replaced.

**Watch for:** modules drift apart on hard floors — look for connecting clips, or budget for a large rug to anchor them (see our [rug guide](/blog/rug-size-small-living-room/)). And per-module pricing adds up fast; a 3-module setup often costs more than an equivalent regular sofa.

## The delivery measurement everyone forgets

Before ordering ANY sofa, measure:

1. **Doorway width** (and every hallway/turn between the street and the room)
2. **The sofa's diagonal depth** — from the top-back corner to the bottom-front corner. This is the number that must clear the doorway when angled through, and it's always smaller than you'd guess.
3. **Elevator dimensions**, if applicable — the number one sofa-delivery killer in apartment buildings.

If the diagonal depth exceeds the narrowest doorway, you need: removable legs (buys you 10–15 cm), a modular sofa (#5, arrives in pieces), or a different sofa. Discovering this on delivery day — with the truck idling outside — is an expensive, sweaty lesson. Ask any furniture delivery driver; they've seen it weekly.

## What to avoid (the no-buy list)

- **Standard 220 cm three-seaters.** Built for 4-meter walls. In a small room they leave no walkway and no side table.
- **Recliners and reclining sofas.** The mechanism adds 15–20 cm of depth and needs 60 cm behind to recline. A manual armchair reclines better in less space.
- **Skirted bases.** They hide the floor and visually anchor the sofa like a boulder. Legs, always legs.
- **Oversized rolled arms.** Each 25 cm arm is 25 cm you're not sitting on. Track arms or slim slopes only.
- **Deep "cloud" sofas (110 cm+).** Glorious to sink into, devastating to small rooms — and hard to get out of, which matters more than you'd think.

## The takeaway

Rank your needs: everyday seating → apartment sofa; tiny room → loveseat + chair; lounger → chaise-end; guests → sleeper; mover/rearranger → modular. Then apply the filters: 170–200 cm wide, under 95 cm deep, slim arms, visible legs, low back — and measure the delivery path before you fall in love. Do that and the biggest object in the room becomes its best asset instead of its biggest regret.

## Frequently asked questions

**What size sofa is best for a small living room?**
170–200 cm wide and 85–95 cm deep, with slim arms (under 15 cm) and visible legs. This "apartment sofa" scale seats three without killing walkways. Standard 220 cm three-seaters are too big for most small rooms.

**Is a sectional too big for a small living room?**
Usually yes — but a chaise-end sofa (#3) gives you the sectional's best seat at two-thirds the footprint. If you must have a sectional, keep it under 230 cm on the long side and skip the corner unit.

**Loveseat or apartment sofa for a small room?**
If your wall fits 190 cm, take the apartment sofa — you'll use the third seat constantly. Choose the loveseat only when the wall is under 185 cm or you specifically want the loveseat + armchair arrangement.

**How do I know if a sofa will fit through my door?**
Measure the sofa's diagonal depth (top-back to bottom-front corner) — it must be smaller than your narrowest doorway width. Also measure hallways, turns, and elevators. Removable legs buy 10–15 cm; modular sofas arrive in pieces.

**What sofa color makes a small living room look bigger?**
Mid-tone neutrals (greige, warm gray, oatmeal) recede best — very dark sofas become visual boulders, very white ones show everything. Match the sofa's undertone to your wall color (see our [paint guide](/blog/best-paint-colors-small-living-room/)) for a cohesive, larger-feeling room.
