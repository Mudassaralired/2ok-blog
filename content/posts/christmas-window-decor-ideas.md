A decorated Christmas window works twice: it glows inside the room all evening, and out to the street after dark. No other decoration gives both views for the same effort, which is why the window deserves its own plan rather than leftover mantel garland. The best windows are not the most crowded; they have one clear idea, lit well, at the right size for the glass.

Most window decor articles list ideas without teaching any of them: no ribbon lengths, no wreath sizes, no mounting method that survives a month on glass, and almost no safety advice for lights near curtains. This guide fixes that, with fifteen ideas taught properly, sizing by window type, costs, renter-safe mounting, and safety rules to read before you plug anything in.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - A window wreath should span one half to two thirds of the window width: 40 to 50 centimetres for a standard window.
> - Suction hooks hold up to 2 kilograms on clean, smooth glass, but fail on textured or condensation-covered glass.
> - Battery fairy lights with a timer are the safest window light: no cords near curtains and no outlet overload.
> - Never block an emergency exit window with a fixed decoration, and keep real candles 30 centimetres from curtains.
> - The most photogenic night window is a lit silhouette village on the sill or a wreath with fairy lights.

## Measure and plan your window first

Measure the visible glass width and height, note the frame type — wood, uPVC or metal — and whether the window opens inward, outward or not at all. The frame decides mounting, and the opening direction decides what hangs where: a decoration on an inward-opening casement swings with the window. Also note sill depth: a village, candles or mini tree needs at least 12 to 15 centimetres.

Check the light next. A street-facing window's evening audience is outside, so its decoration should read as a silhouette. A kitchen window is viewed close up, so it suits delicate ornaments and snowflakes. Decide the audience first: a snowflake curtain magical from the sofa is invisible from the street.

Finally, find the nearest outlet and count what already runs from it: a tree, a mantel display and two windows on one extension lead overloads outlets fast. Battery lights with timers solve most window lighting, and they are the recommendation throughout this guide.

## Sizing by window type

A standard window, roughly 90 to 120 centimetres wide, suits a wreath 40 to 50 centimetres across, a garland frame of 2.5 to 3 metres, or six to eight hanging ornaments at staggered heights. A picture window over 150 centimetres needs a wreath of 60 centimetres or larger, or two smaller wreaths side by side. A small apartment window, 60 to 80 centimetres, suits a wreath 30 to 35 centimetres across, or a single hanging cluster.

Bay windows are decorated in sections: one wreath or cluster per pane, garland along the top pelmet tying them together. A kitchen window suits hanging ornaments at staggered heights, and a deep sill is itself the display — a village, candles or mini trees — with glass clear above for daylight.

Hang ornaments and snowflakes with the lowest point at least 15 centimetres above the sill and the highest at least 10 centimetres below the frame top. Vary heights by 10 to 20 centimetres between neighbours; identical heights look like a shop display, staggered heights look handmade.

## 15 Christmas window decor ideas

Costs are approximate; battery lights with timers recommended.

### 1. Garland framed window with fairy lights

Evergreen garland around all four sides of the frame, woven with warm white battery fairy lights. Cost around £15 to £30. Time one hour. Fix with removable hooks, never nails into uPVC.

### 2. Lit wreath in the centre of the window

One [wreath](/blog/diy-christmas-wreath-ideas/), sized as above, hung from a suction hook or a ribbon over the frame, with battery lights woven through it. Cost around £12 to £25. Time 20 minutes. A ribbon over the frame holds more weight than a suction hook on cold glass.

### 3. Lit silhouette village on the sill

Card or wooden house silhouettes on the sill, backlit by fairy lights behind them. Cost around £10 to £20, or under £5 if you cut the houses from card. Time one to two hours. Full tutorial below.

### 4. Paper snowflake curtain

Paper snowflakes in three sizes, hung on clear thread at staggered heights. Cost around £3 to £6. Time two hours for fifteen to twenty snowflakes. Cut them 12 to 20 centimetres across so they read from across the room.

### 5. Hanging ornaments at staggered heights

Baubles, pinecones or dried oranges hung on ribbons or clear thread from a rod above the window. Cost around £8 to £18. Time one hour. Keep each ornament under 100 grams so it hangs straight.

### 6. Dried orange and greenery garland across the top

A swag of greenery with dried orange slices and cinnamon sticks, across the top of the window only. Cost around £10 to £18. Time one hour plus drying. Dry orange slices at 100 degrees for two to three hours, turning once.

### 7. Red bows on every pane

One large velvet or wired-ribbon bow per pane, at the centre or top corner. Cost around £8 to £15 for three to four bows. Time 30 minutes.

### 8. Chalk marker winter scene on the glass

A hand-drawn village, trees or snowfall on the inside of the glass with a chalk marker, wiped off in January. Cost around £5 to £8. Time one to two hours. Test on a corner first, and use a chalk marker, not a permanent paint pen.

### 9. Two wreaths, one inside and one outside

Matching wreaths on both sides of the same window, reading from street and room. Cost around £20 to £40. Time 30 minutes. Fix the outside wreath to the frame, not the glass, so wind cannot peel it off.

### 10. Small wreaths across a wide window

Three or four small wreaths, 25 to 30 centimetres each, in a row across a picture window. Cost around £18 to £30. Time 30 minutes. Space them 15 to 20 centimetres apart.

### 11. Window box dressed for Christmas

A window box filled with evergreen sprigs, pinecones, red berries and battery lights. Cost around £12 to £22. Time one hour. Water lightly once a week so greenery does not drop needles early.

### 12. Mini tree beside the window

A small potted tree, 60 to 90 centimetres, beside the window with its own lights, reading as one display with the window. Cost around £15 to £35. Time 20 minutes. Keep it clear of curtains and radiators.

### 13. Pink paper chain window

A paper chain in pink, cream and gold across the window for a non-traditional look. Cost around £4 to £8. Time one hour. Make links 15 centimetres long so the chain drapes.

### 14. Battery candles in jars on the sill

Battery candles in jars or lanterns along the sill, on a timer for the evening. Cost around £10 to £20 for three to five candles. Time 15 minutes. Never substitute real candles near curtains.

### 15. Lit Santa or star silhouette

A flat silhouette — Santa in a sleigh, a large star, a nativity shape — cut from card or plywood, backlit on the glass at night. Cost around £8 to £15 for card. Time two hours. Keep it within the glass area so its edges glow cleanly.

## Step by step lit silhouette village window

Cut house silhouettes from thick card in three heights — 12, 18 and 25 centimetres — with pitched roofs and windows cut as small rectangles or arches. Five to seven houses fill a standard sill; three fill a small one. Cover the card in one colour — kraft, white or deep green — and stand houses along the back of the sill, tallest in the middle, with bottle-brush trees between them.

Run warm white battery fairy lights behind the houses, not in front, so light shines through the cut windows and around rooflines. Set the timer for 5pm to 11pm, so the village glows after dark. From the street it reads as a glowing skyline; from inside, as a warm display. Cost under £20, time one to two hours.

## Renter safe mounting on glass and frames

Suction hooks hold about 2 kilograms on clean, smooth glass, but fail on textured glass, condensation-covered glass, and in cold weather when the seal contracts. Clean the glass, dry it fully, press the hook firmly, and test with the intended weight for 24 hours before trusting it with a wreath. For heavier wreaths, hang from a ribbon over the frame top, fixed inside with a removable hook: the frame carries the weight, not the glass.

Removable adhesive hooks work on wood and uPVC frames, but never on freshly painted frames or below the wreath's weight rating. Chalk scenes and snowflakes need only a small hook or tension rod, and come down in minutes in January. ## Outdoor and street facing windows

An outside window faces wind, rain and temperature swings, so decorations need outdoor-rated fixings and weatherproof lights, with the battery box protected from rain under the sill overhang. Fix garlands with outdoor hooks or cable ties, never adhesive that cold and damp defeat. Check after the first storm: a wreath that survives its first windy night usually survives the season.

Fresh greenery lasts two to three weeks outdoors in cold weather, but drops needles faster in wind, so check it weekly. Paper decorations belong indoors only: one rainy night destroys them. For porch ideas, see the [christmas front door decor](/blog/christmas-front-door-decor/) guide.

## Window Christmas safety rules

Windows combine the season's three risks: electricity, flame and blocked exits. Never block an emergency exit window with a fixed decoration: anything on an egress window must come down in seconds without tools. Keep real candles at least 30 centimetres from curtains, [garlands](/blog/christmas-garland-ideas/) and paper, and never leave them burning unattended — battery candles give the same glow with none of the risk.

For lights, battery lights with timers are best, removing cords entirely. For plug-in lights, never run cords under rugs, through closing window frames, or from an outlet already loaded with a tree and [mantel](/blog/christmas-mantel-decor/) display. Check strings for damaged wires before hanging, replace cracked insulation, and switch lights off overnight even with a timer. Keep ornaments light and low: a falling bauble shatters, and a heavy ornament swinging in a draught can crack a pane.

## Common mistakes and fixes

The most common mistake is a wreath too small for the window: a 30-centimetre wreath on a 150-centimetre pane looks lost, so size up to 60 centimetres or use a row of small wreaths. Second, suction hooks failing on cold or textured glass: clean and dry the glass, test for 24 hours, or switch to ribbon over the frame. Third, cords trailing across curtains or under rugs: switch to battery lights.

Fourth, blocking an exit window: move the display to a non-egress window, or make it removable in seconds. Fifth, paper outdoors: one rainy night destroys it, so keep paper inside. Sixth, forgetting one audience: street-facing windows need bold silhouettes, kitchen windows need delicate close-up details.

## FAQ

**What size wreath should I hang in a window?**
One half to two thirds of the window width: 40 to 50 centimetres for a standard 90 to 120 centimetre window, 60 centimetres or more for a large picture window, 30 to 35 centimetres for a small apartment window.

**How do I hang a wreath on a window without damaging it?**
Use a suction hook on clean, dry, smooth glass, tested for 24 hours, or a ribbon looped over the frame top and fixed inside with a removable hook. The ribbon method holds heavier wreaths and works on textured glass.

**Are battery fairy lights safe in a window?**
Yes, they are the safest window light: no mains cords near curtains, no outlet overload, and a timer switches them off. Use outdoor-rated lights and protect the battery box from rain outside.

**Can I decorate a window in a rental?**
Yes. Suction hooks, ribbon over the frame, removable frame hooks, chalk marker scenes and paper snowflakes on thread all come down without damage. Avoid nails in uPVC frames and adhesive on fresh paint.

**Should Christmas window decor face inside or outside?**
Decide by audience: street-facing windows suit bold silhouettes and lit wreaths that read from the pavement, while windows viewed close up suit delicate ornaments and snowflakes. A lit wreath or village does both.

**When should I take Christmas window decor down?**
In the first week of January, before dust and sun fade set in. Label lights by window, store wreaths in shape-holding boxes, and keep silhouette houses and their lights together for next year.

A Christmas window needs one clear idea, sized to the glass, lit safely and mounted to survive until January. Pick the wreath or the village, hang it properly, set the timer, and the window will do the rest.
