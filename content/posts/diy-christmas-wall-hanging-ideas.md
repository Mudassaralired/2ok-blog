A Christmas wall hanging is the fastest way to make a room feel festive without buying a tree or moving furniture: one handmade piece, hung from a single hook, turns a blank wall into the warmest corner of the house. These 15 DIY Christmas wall hanging ideas cover every skill level — each with a real cost, time, and honest hanging rules.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - Most hangings cost £3 to £15 and take under an hour.
> - Match the hardware to the weight: adhesive hooks hold up to 1 kg.
> - Renters can hang everything here with adhesive hooks, a tension rod or a door hook.
> - Weigh every finished piece on kitchen scales before you hang it, and never hang it above a bed or chair.
> - Store hangings flat in tissue paper and they will last five Christmases or more.

## Felt and Fabric Christmas Wall Hangings

### 1. Classic felt Christmas banner

Felt triangles, or a panel with cut-out trees, stitched or glued onto a dowel: the most pinned Christmas wall hanging on Pinterest, and it costs almost nothing. Cut with sharp fabric scissors — ragged edges make a banner look cheap. Cost: £5 to £8. Time: 1 to 2 hours. Difficulty: easy. Hang it from two adhesive hooks; felt never frays, so no hemming is needed.

### 2. Felt tree wall hanging with pockets

Five felt triangles in descending sizes on a backing panel make a flat Christmas tree — and the triangles double as pockets for treats or notes. The most forgiving felt project — uneven stitching reads as charming. Cost: £6 to £9. Time: 2 hours. Difficulty: easy. Use a dark green panel about 60 × 90 cm and hang it from a dowel.

### 3. Macramé Christmas wall hanging

Macramé is the slow luxury of this list: cotton cord knotted into a tree, a star, or a fringe panel with beads. A basic tree uses only square knots and lark's head knots, both learnable in ten minutes, and 3 mm cord is the most forgiving. Cost: £8 to £14. Time: 3 to 4 hours. Difficulty: moderate. Work on a dowel at eye level. The [macramé wall hanging](/blog/macrame-wall-hanging) guide covers the knots.

### 4. Fabric scrap bunting

Cut triangles from leftover Christmas fabric — old curtains, a tablecloth, a charity-shop shirt — and sew or glue them onto bias tape. The charm is in the mix: three or four patterns sharing one colour look deliberate, not random. Cost: £3 to £6. Time: 1 hour. Difficulty: easy. Twelve flags span about 2.5 m, which fits a wall above a sofa. Iron every flag first.

### 5. Embroidered hoop Christmas hanging

One embroidery hoop, 20 to 30 cm across, with a stitched snowflake, star or "Merry Christmas" in backstitch, is the smallest hanging here and the quickest gift. Stretch cotton or linen in the hoop, stitch your design, then trim the excess fabric and glue it behind the inner ring. Cost: £4 to £7. Time: 1 to 2 hours. Difficulty: easy. Hang three hoops at different heights, and keep the stitches loose.

## Natural and Rustic Christmas Wall Hangings

### 6. Wood-slice Christmas tree wall hanging

Glue or screw wood slices in a triangle — one at the top, then rows of two, three and four — onto a board or hessian, and you have a rustic tree that hangs flat on the wall. Slices 5 to 8 cm across work best. Cost: £8 to £12. Time: 2 hours plus drying time. Difficulty: easy. Seal the slices with clear matt varnish so they do not crack, and use a hot-glue gun for the lightest slices or small screws for anything over 200 g.

### 7. Driftwood branch star and ornament hanger

A straight branch, 80 to 120 cm long, hung by twine from two hooks, becomes a shelf in the air for paper stars, dried orange slices, cinnamon sticks and small baubles. It is the most flexible hanging here — rearrange it every year without making anything new. Cost: £3 to £7. Time: 45 minutes. Difficulty: easy. Scrub the branch, sand off splinters, and hang it level; a branch that slopes even slightly looks like a mistake.

### 8. Dried orange and cinnamon hanging

Thread dried orange slices, cinnamon sticks and star anise onto twine and hang the strands from a dowel or branch. It smells wonderful, costs almost nothing, and dries in the oven at 100°C for two to three hours — slice the oranges 5 mm thick, or they curl or take a day to dry. Cost: £3 to £5. Time: 30 minutes plus drying. Difficulty: easy. Hang five or six 40 to 60 cm strands side by side so they read as one piece.

### 9. Pinecone and greenery wall swag

Gather pinecones, a few sprigs of pine or fir, and twine, then tie the cones in a loose cluster with the greenery fanning out behind them. It is the only hanging here that smells of the season the moment it goes up, and it takes twenty minutes from a walk in the park to a finished piece. Cost: £2 to £4. Time: 20 minutes. Difficulty: easy. Bake pinecones at 100°C for 30 minutes first — it opens them fully and kills anything living inside — and tie the twine through the scales so the cones hang point-down.

### 10. Rustic twig star wall hanging

Five straight twigs, each about 40 cm long, tied at the corners with jute twine, make a five-pointed star for the wall. Wrap the joints tightly with twine and hot glue, then hang a small bauble or a sprig of holly from the centre. Cost: £2 to £4. Time: 30 minutes. Difficulty: easy. Choose twigs of similar thickness — one fat arm looks lopsided — and spray the finished star with clear matt sealant so it survives storage. It weighs under 300 g.

## Paper, Wood and Hoop Christmas Wall Hangings

### 11. Paper star hanging mobile

Cut five-pointed stars from thick card, fold each one along its points, and hang them at staggered heights from a hoop or dowel. Mixed gold, cream and deep red stars catch the light best, and the piece moves gently in a draught. Cost: £3 to £5. Time: 1 to 2 hours. Difficulty: easy. Use 200 gsm card or heavier — printer paper droops within days — and hang the stars on clear thread so they appear to float.

### 12. Wooden bead Christmas tree hanging

Thread large wooden beads onto cord in a triangle — rows of one, two, three and four beads, each on its own line tied to two vertical cords — and you have a flat beaded tree that folds away to nothing in January. The tidiest hanging to store: beads do not care about uneven spacing. Cost: £6 to £10. Time: 1 hour. Difficulty: easy. Use 20 mm beads for the main rows and one 30 mm bead as the star, knotting the cord firmly after each row so the beads cannot slide.

### 13. Advent countdown wall hanging

Sew or glue 24 small numbered pockets or bags onto a backing panel in a grid, and the hanging becomes the family's countdown calendar. Each pocket holds a chocolate or a note; write the numbers in gold pen on kraft paper tags. Cost: £8 to £15. Time: 3 hours. Difficulty: moderate. Make the pockets 7 × 10 cm so 24 of them fit on a 60 × 80 cm panel, and hang the panel from a dowel through a top hem — loaded, it weighs close to 1 kg, so use two hooks, not one.

### 14. Woven wall hanging with Christmas colours

A simple frame loom — or a picture frame with nails around the edge — woven with cream, red and green yarn makes a soft textured hanging that looks far more expensive than it is. Weave chunky and fine yarn in turns for contrast, and finish with a fringe cut to one length. Cost: £7 to £12. Time: 3 to 4 hours. Difficulty: moderate. Keep the tension even: tight weaving pulls the frame inwards, loose weaving sags. At 400 to 600 g, one picture hook holds it — the [DIY woven wall hanging ideas](/blog/diy-woven-wall-hanging-ideas) guide covers looms and finishing.

### 15. Hanging Christmas village silhouette

Cut a skyline of houses, a church and pine trees from black card, mount it on a board painted cream or pale gold, and hang it with fairy lights behind the top edge. At dusk the silhouette glows, and it doubles as a night light in a hallway. Cost: £6 to £10. Time: 2 hours. Difficulty: moderate. Cut the skyline in one piece so it reads as a single street, use a craft knife and a metal ruler for clean rooflines, and keep the lights behind the board.

## Hanging Hardware by Weight: What Holds What

Every hanging here weighs under 2 kg: you never need a wall plug or a stud finder. Weigh the finished piece on kitchen scales, then choose hardware rated for at least twice that weight. Adhesive hooks come in two bands: small hooks hold up to 450 g, large ones up to about 1 kg, and both need a clean, dry wall and a full hour to bond before loading. Picture hooks hold 1 to 2 kg and leave one tiny hole that filler covers in seconds.

Two hooks beat one for anything wider than 60 cm or heavier than 500 g: the load splits and the piece hangs level. On plasterboard, adhesive hooks are safest; nails hold poorly and pull out under sustained load. On brick or solid plaster, a picture hook is the simplest fix. Whatever the wall, never hang a piece above a bed, a cot or a chair — move it to the side wall instead.

## Renter Friendly Hanging: No Holes, No Damage

Renters can make and hang every idea in this guide without losing a deposit. Adhesive hooks and strips are the default: stick them to clean painted walls, wait an hour, hang, and remove them in January by pulling the tab slowly down the wall — never outwards, which is what peels paint. A tension rod in a window frame or alcove holds light hangings from S-hooks, and a door hook takes a banner in seconds.

For heavier pieces, a picture-rail hook is the renter's best friend: hooks slide along the rail and hang the piece on a cord, with no wall contact at all. Avoid tape on painted walls — masking tape and packing tape both leave residue and pull paint after a month, and double-sided foam tape is worse. The [renter-friendly wall decor](/blog/renter-friendly-wall-decor) and [damage-free wall decor](/blog/damage-free-wall-decor) guides cover the full range of no-hole methods.

## Takedown and Storage: Keeping Hangings for Next Christmas

Take everything down in the first week of January, before dust and damp settle in. Photograph each hanging in place first. Remove adhesive hooks by the pull tab and fill nail holes with white filler. Felt and fabric hangings roll, never fold: roll them around a cardboard tube with tissue paper between the layers. Paper stars and mobiles go flat in a tissue-lined box, each layer separated by card. Dried orange slices go in a sealed bag with a silica gel sachet, or they attract damp.

Label every box with the hanging's name and room, and store the boxes flat, not under heavy things — a crushed felt banner never recovers its shape. Kept this way, every hanging here lasts at least five Christmases, which brings a £8 banner down to under £2 a year. Check each piece next year before it goes back up.

## FAQ

### What is the easiest DIY Christmas wall hanging for a beginner?

A felt banner or a paper star hanging. Both need only scissors, glue and a dowel, cost under £8, and take one to two hours. The felt banner is most forgiving: felt does not fray.

### How do I hang one without damaging the wall?

Use adhesive hooks rated for twice the hanging's weight, stick them to a clean dry wall, wait an hour before loading, and remove them by pulling the tab slowly down the wall. Over 1 kg, use two hooks, or a picture hook, which leaves one tiny hole filler covers in seconds.

### How heavy can a hanging be before it needs a nail?

Adhesive hooks hold up to about 1 kg, so most felt, paper and fabric hangings never need a nail. Anything over 1 kg — a loaded advent calendar or a wood-slice tree — belongs on picture hooks or a screw hook. Weigh the piece first, and use hardware rated for twice its weight.

### Can I hang these in a rented home?

Yes — adhesive hooks, a tension rod, a door hook or a picture rail all work, and none leaves a mark. Avoid tape on painted walls, which peels paint, and never use double-sided foam tape.

### How do I store a wall hanging so it lasts?

Roll felt and fabric hangings around a cardboard tube with tissue between the layers, lay paper stars flat in a tissue-lined box, and keep dried orange slices in a sealed bag with a silica gel sachet. Label the box and store it flat.
