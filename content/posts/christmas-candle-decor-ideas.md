> - Every idea here costs $4 to $35, and most take under an hour.

## Candlelit Mantels: The Warmest Glow in the Room

### 1. DIY frosted glass hurricane

A frosted hurricane turns a plain pillar candle into a soft glowing lantern for almost nothing. You need a tall glass vase, frosted-glass spray and a white pillar. Cost: $8 to $12. Time: 30 minutes plus drying. Difficulty: easy.

1. Clean the glass inside and out, then dry it completely.
2. Spray two light coats outside, ten minutes apart.
3. Leave it to dry for two hours.
4. Set the candle inside on a small plate; light it only when the glass is fully dry.

### 2. Pillar trio with fresh greenery

Three white pillars of different heights, set along the mantel with pine sprigs tucked between them, is the simplest mantel look here — and the one guests photograph most. Cost: $12 to $20. Time: 20 minutes. Difficulty: easy. Use flameless pillars if the garland sits close to the candles. The [Christmas mantel decor](/blog/christmas-mantel-decor) guide pairs this look with stockings and garland.

### 3. Brass lantern pair at the mantel ends

Two matching brass lanterns, one at each end of the mantel, frame the whole display. Choose lanterns 25 to 30 cm tall, with a real pillar candle in each. Cost: $18 to $35. Time: 10 minutes. Difficulty: easy. The glass shields the flame and the base catches any wax.

## The Christmas Table: Greenery Trays and Taper Candles

### 4. DIY cinnamon-stick-wrapped pillar

A plain pillar candle wrapped in cinnamon sticks looks like a shop-bought luxury and smells wonderful before it is even lit. You need a thick pillar, cinnamon sticks, twine and a hot glue gun. Cost: $6 to $10. Time: 30 minutes. Difficulty: easy.

1. Stand the sticks upright around the candle, trimmed to its height.
2. Tie twine twice around the middle to hold them in place.
3. Add a dab of hot glue behind two or three sticks for extra grip.
4. Never light it without a hurricane glass around it — the sticks are flammable.

### 5. Greenery tray centerpiece

A long wooden tray filled with fresh greenery, five taper candles in brass holders and a few votives is the classic Christmas table centerpiece, running the full length of the table. Cost: $15 to $25. Time: 30 minutes. Difficulty: easy. Set the tray on a runner so wax drips land on wood, not linen. The [Christmas table decor](/blog/christmas-table-decor) guide builds the full place settings around it; the [DIY Christmas centerpiece ideas](/blog/diy-christmas-centerpiece-ideas) guide offers more tray variations.

### 6. DIY bauble-base candle display

Set a short pillar candle on a bed of small baubles in a wide glass bowl, and the baubles catch the candlelight like a tiny chandelier. You need a wide shallow bowl, 12 to 15 small baubles in two colors, and a short pillar on a small plate. Cost: $9 to $15. Time: 20 minutes. Difficulty: easy.

1. Fill the bowl with baubles, leaving a flat space in the center.
2. Set a small ceramic plate in the center, then the candle on it.
3. Tuck greenery between the baubles at the edges.
4. Use a flameless pillar here — real flame above glass baubles is a bad combination.

## Windows and Light: Candles That Glow from the Street

### 7. Window taper row

A row of white tapers in brass holders along a deep windowsill is the oldest Christmas window look, and it still beats almost everything else. Space five to seven evenly along the sill, all the same height, so the row reads as one clean line from outside. Cost: $10 to $20. Time: 15 minutes. Difficulty: easy. Use flameless tapers with a timer — real flames on a windowsill sit too close to curtains. The [Christmas window decor ideas](/blog/christmas-window-decor-ideas) guide pairs the candle row with wreaths and garland.

### 8. DIY mason-jar luminaries

Mason jars with a tea light inside and a ribbon around the neck make the prettiest window lights, and they cost pennies. You need two or three mason jars, tea lights, twine or ribbon, and faux snow or Epsom salt. Cost: $4 to $8. Time: 20 minutes. Difficulty: easy.

1. Spoon 2 cm of faux snow or Epsom salt into each jar.
2. Set a tea light on the snow, pressing it down gently.
3. Tie twine or ribbon around the neck of each jar.
4. Line the jars along the sill, 20 cm apart.

The [DIY mason jar wall decor](/blog/diy-mason-jar-wall-decor) guide uses the same jars in a different way.

### 9. Battery candles in every window

One flameless candle in every front window, all switched on at dusk, makes the whole house glow from the street. Buy six matching flameless tapers with a remote and timer, set to the same six-hour timer so they light together every evening. Cost: $12 to $22. Time: 10 minutes. Difficulty: easy. No flame near curtains, no wax on the sill.

## Stairs, Entry and Hallways: Lantern-Lit Arrivals

### 10. Lantern-lit staircase

Line the steps of your staircase with lanterns, one on every other tread, and the stairs become the warmest path in the house. Use two sizes — tall on the lower steps, short higher up — with a flameless pillar in each. Cost: $20 to $30. Time: 20 minutes. Difficulty: easy. Push each lantern against the wall side of the tread so nobody kicks it.

### 11. Entry console candle cluster

The entry console is the first thing guests see, so give it a cluster: one tall pillar, two votives and a small lantern on a round wooden tray with pine sprigs. Cost: $11 to $20. Time: 15 minutes. Difficulty: easy. Keep the cluster to one end so keys and post still have a home.

### 12. Stair garland with tealights

Drape a pine garland along the banister and nest small glass tealight holders into it every 40 cm. The glass protects the greenery from the flame, and the lights follow the line of the stairs. Cost: $10 to $18. Time: 30 minutes. Difficulty: easy. Wire each holder to the banister so it cannot slide.

## Quiet Corners: Bedrooms, Bathrooms and Reading Nooks

### 13. DIY advent candle display

An advent display counts down the days to Christmas with four candles, and it doubles as the prettiest thing on the sideboard. You need four pillars in red, cream or white, a long wooden board or tray, greenery and four small number tags. Cost: $10 to $16. Time: 40 minutes. Difficulty: easy.

1. Space the four candles evenly along the board, 10 cm apart.
2. Tuck greenery around the bases.
3. Tie a numbered tag to each candle with twine.
4. Light one more each Sunday of Advent, never more than one at a time.

### 14. Bedroom bedside candle pair

Two small candles on the bedside table — one scented, one plain — make winter evenings feel calm and slow. Choose a scented jar candle in pine or cinnamon and a plain votive, both in glass, on a small plate. Cost: $8 to $15. Time: 5 minutes. Difficulty: easy. Blow them out before you sleep, every night, without exception.

### 15. Bathroom spa candle tray

Turn the bath into a spa with a wooden bath tray holding three candles — a large jar, a votive and a tealight — plus a sprig of eucalyptus. Cost: $9 to $15. Time: 10 minutes. Difficulty: easy. Keep every candle an arm's length from the water and towels — a tray with a lip is the only safe spot.

## The Safe Candle System: Flameless vs Real Flame

Candle safety is a system: the first decision is which kind of candle goes where. Real flame belongs only where you are awake, present and watching: the living room, the dining table during dinner, the bathroom during a bath. Flameless candles belong everywhere else — windows, stairs, hallways, the entry and children's rooms.

December is the peak month for candle fires, according to fire-safety guidance: candles burn longer and closer to decorations than at any other time of year. Hence the 30 cm rule: keep every flame at least 30 cm (12 inches) from anything that can burn.

Never leave a flame unattended: blow candles out when you leave the room, when you go to bed, and when the last person leaves the house. A timer on flameless candles does this job for you. Trim every wick to about 5 mm before lighting.

| Zone | Best candle | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mantel and dining table | Real flame | You can see it |
| Windows | Flameless, timer | Near curtains |
| Stairs and hallways | Flameless | People walk past in the dark |
| Entry console | Flameless | Coats pass through |
| Bedroom | Real flame, awake | Blow it out before you sleep |
| Children's rooms | Flameless only | No flame where children play |

## The Three-Height Grouping Rule

A single candle looks lonely, and a row of identical candles looks like a shop shelf. The fix is the three-height rule: group candles in threes, each at a different height. A tall pillar, a medium jar and a short votive read as one natural arrangement.

Put the tallest candle at the back or center, then step down by 8 to 10 cm for each smaller candle. On a mantel, the tall candle sits in the middle with the two shorter ones flanking it; on a tray, it goes at one end.

The rule also keeps displays safe: heights spread the flames apart, so no two flames sit close enough to heat each other. Buy three sizes of the same color.

## Candle Types and Scent Guide

Pillars are the workhorses: they burn for hours, stand on their own, and suit mantels, trays and lanterns. Tapers are the elegant ones, best in brass holders on the table or windowsill, but they drip, so use a holder with a wide cup. Votives and tea lights are the fillers — cheap and small, perfect for garlands and jars, but they burn out in two to four hours. Jar candles are the safest real-flame option because the glass contains the wax and the flame.

Pine and fir smell like the tree itself and suit the living room and entry. Cinnamon and clove are warm and spicy, best on the dining table. Orange and cranberry are bright and fresh, good for the bathroom and kitchen. One scented candle per room is enough — two scents in one room fight each other.

| Candle type | Best for | Burn time | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pillar | Mantel, trays, lanterns | 40–80 hours | Needs a plate |
| Taper | Table, windowsill | 6–10 hours | Drips |
| Votive | Garlands, jars | 2–4 hours | Burns out fast |
| Jar candle | Bedroom, bathroom | 25–50 hours | Glass gets hot |

## After Christmas: Reusing Every Candle and Holder

Christmas candles do not have to end on Boxing Day. Half-burned pillars become everyday candles for the rest of winter: trim the wick, level the top with a warm knife, and burn them through January and February. Leftover tapers work all year in brass holders.

The holders are the real keepers. Glass hurricanes and mason jars work in spring with tulips, in summer with sand, and in autumn with dried leaves. Brass candlesticks and lanterns never go out of style, and wooden trays become everyday serving pieces. Compost the pine sprigs and keep the baubles.

## FAQ

### Can I use real candles on a Christmas mantel?

Yes, if you can see them while they burn and they sit at least 30 cm from greenery and stockings. Use a hurricane glass or lantern to shield the flame, and blow them out when you leave the room. If the garland sits close, use flameless pillars.

### How many candles should I put on a Christmas table?

Five to seven candles suit a table for six to eight: tapers in brass holders and votives tucked into the greenery. Fewer than five looks sparse on a long table, and more than seven crowds the plates.

### Are flameless candles safe in windows?

Yes — flameless candles are the safest window option because there is no flame near curtains and no wax on the sill. Choose ones with a timer so they switch on at dusk and off after six hours, and use warm white LEDs.

### What is the best scent for Christmas candles?

Pine and fir suit the living room, cinnamon and clove suit the dining table after dinner, and orange and cranberry suit the bathroom and kitchen. Use one scented candle per room, and keep dining-table candles unscented while food is served.
