Your front door is the first thing every visitor sees in December — and the smallest surface that makes the biggest difference. A single well-sized wreath can make a plain door look finished, while a tiny wreath on a big door, or garland that blocks the door from closing, makes the whole entrance feel off.

The secret is sizing everything to the door, then decorating in the right order: door first, frame second, flanks last. This guide gives you the sizing tables, fifteen ideas for real doors — single, double, narrow, glass-panelled and apartment — and the hanging and weatherproofing tricks that keep everything in place through wind, rain and frost.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - **Wreath rule:** a wreath should be about **two-thirds of your door's width** — 22–26 inches for a standard 36-inch door
> - **Hang height:** centre the wreath **58–64 inches from the floor**, roughly eye level, not centred on the door panel
> - **Garland length:** measure **up one side, across the top, down the other side**, then add 1–2 feet for swag — about 17 feet for a standard door
> - **Decorate in order:** wreath → garland frame → lanterns and planters → lights → doormat, and stop before it feels crowded
> - **Windproofing:** wire wreaths to their hooks, weight planters, and use outdoor-rated lights on a timer

## Size everything to your door

Most door decor disappoints because it is the wrong size, not the wrong style. A wreath that looked generous in the shop shrinks on a real door; garland bought by guesswork runs out halfway around the frame. Measure first.

| Door width | Wreath diameter | Garland to frame the door |
|---|---|---|
| 30 inches (narrow) | 18–20 inches | About 16 feet |
| 32 inches | 20–22 inches | About 16.5 feet |
| 36 inches (standard) | 22–26 inches | About 17 feet |
| Double doors, 72 inches | One 30–36 inch wreath, or two 24-inch wreaths | About 20 feet |

The garland figure is the door height twice plus the width: for a standard 80-inch door, 80 + 80 + 36 = 196 inches, about 16 feet 4 inches. Add 1–2 feet for a soft swag at the top; two 9-foot garlands joined at the top cover a standard door.

Hang the wreath so its centre sits 58–64 inches from the floor — roughly eye level. Centring it on the door panel itself usually hangs it too high. If your door has a window or panels, centre the wreath on the upper-middle section, not on the glass.

## The door-first decorating order

**1. Pick a palette of two or three colours plus green.** Classic red, cream and brass; modern black, white and green; Scandinavian natural wood, white and soft grey. Check every piece against it — the palette is what makes separate purchases look like one design.

**2. Hang the wreath.** It is the focal point and the scale-setter. Everything else is sized against it.

**3. Frame the door with garland.** Pin or hook garland around the frame first, then shape the swag. Keep garland about 2 inches outside the door edge so the weatherstripping seals and the door swings freely.

**4. Add flanking pieces.** Lanterns, planters or small trees, one on each side or in a single larger grouping. Tallest at the back.

**5. Add lights last, then the doormat.** Lights woven into garland or wrapped around planters, on an outdoor timer so they switch on at dusk. A festive doormat is the finishing touch — choose coir or rubber, not fabric, since it takes the most wear.

Stop when the door reads clearly from the street. If you cannot see the wreath for the garland, you have gone one piece too far. For the wider porch picture — planters, trees, railings and seating — see our [Christmas porch decor](/blog/christmas-porch-decor/) guide.

## 15 Christmas front door decor ideas

### Wreaths

**1. Classic evergreen wreath with a velvet bow.** Fresh or faux evergreen, pinecones, and one large wired velvet bow in deep red, burgundy or forest green. Velvet holds its shape in cold air where satin flops, and this wreath suits nearly every door colour.

**2. Dried orange and cinnamon wreath.** An evergreen base dressed with dried orange slices, cinnamon bundles and star anise. It smells wonderful, costs little if you dry the oranges yourself, and ties beautifully to a natural palette. See our [DIY Christmas ornaments](/blog/diy-christmas-ornaments/) for drying times.

**3. Flocked snowy wreath.** A frosted, snow-dusted wreath on a dark door — black, navy or deep green — where the white flocking pops. On a white door, flocking disappears, so choose plain evergreen instead.

**4. Berry and magnolia wreath.** Magnolia leaves with red berries and a gold bow, for an elegant look that survives mild winters well. Magnolia leaves curl as they dry, so faux magnolia is the safer buy.

### Garland and swags

**5. Full garland frame with lights.** Cedar or mixed evergreen garland around the whole frame, woven with warm white lights and finished with a bow or bauble cluster at each top corner. Cedar's soft, drooping branches look more natural than stiff pine. Keep one element simple — if the garland is lit and dressed, keep the wreath plain.

**6. Door swag instead of a wreath.** A teardrop swag — greenery gathered at the top and fanning downward — suits narrow doors and glass-panelled doors where a round wreath crowds the window. Hang it from the same hook at the same height.

**7. Asymmetric garland.** Garland that runs up one side and across the top, ending in a long, loose tail on one side, for a collected, modern look. This works best on wider doors where the asymmetry has room to breathe.

### Ribbons, bows and door dressing

**8. Ribbon down the door.** A wide wired ribbon running vertically down the centre of the door, crossed by a horizontal ribbon to form a giant gift bow, with the wreath at the crossing point. Striking, cheap, and removable in minutes. Use wired ribbon — it holds its shape outdoors.

**9. Oversized bow on the wreath.** Skip extra decorations and let one enormous bow do the work: a 10–12 inch wired bow in plaid or velvet transforms a plain evergreen wreath into a statement piece.

**10. Hanging ornaments from the door frame.** Small shatterproof baubles hung at varied lengths from the top of the frame, like a curtain of ornaments. Keep them above head height — glass baubles over a walkway are a hazard when they fall.

### Flanks, lights and finishing touches

**11. Lantern pair.** Two black or brass lanterns flanking the door, filled with battery candles on timers. Tuck a sprig of greenery on top of each to tie them to the wreath. Lanterns give evening glow without any wiring.

**12. Evergreen planters.** A pair of planters with fresh or faux evergreen branches, pinecones and red berries. Weight the base with a paver or sand so wind cannot tip them.

**13. Small porch trees.** Two slim, pre-lit trees flanking the door, 3–4 feet tall, for porches with the space. On a small porch, one tree beats two crowded ones.

**14. Festive doormat layered over a larger mat.** A coir Christmas mat layered over a larger plain outdoor rug. Layering looks intentional, protects the festive mat, and gives visitors a proper wiping surface.

**15. Illuminated star or snowflake above the door.** A lit star or snowflake hung beneath the porch roof, centred above the door, for a finishing glow visible from the street. Battery versions avoid trailing cables.

## Tricky doors: double, narrow, glass and apartment

**Double doors** need symmetry or a single statement. Either hang one oversized wreath (30–36 inches) on one door only — check the doors still open — or hang two matching 24-inch wreaths, one per door, at identical heights. Matching garland across the full frame ties both doors together.

**Narrow doors** (30 inches or less) suit a swag better than a wreath, or a smaller 18–20 inch wreath. Decorate vertically: wreath, a hanging lantern from the porch ceiling, and one planter instead of a pair, so the walkway stays clear.

**Glass-panelled doors** need light pieces that do not block the glass or rattle against it: a swag, a lightweight faux wreath on an over-door hook, and garland on the frame rather than the door. Avoid suction-cup hooks on cold glass — they fail when temperatures drop.

**Apartment doors** (no porch, hallway outside) follow the same rules at smaller scale: a wreath on an over-door hook, a slim garland on the frame if the hallway allows, and nothing on the floor that blocks the corridor. Check building rules first — some blocks restrict hallway decorations for fire-safety reasons. ## Hanging without damage

The right hanger depends on the door. Over-door wreath hooks work on most standard doors and need no fixings — check the door still closes fully, since thick hooks can stop a door latching. Adhesive hooks rated for outdoor use suit smooth, painted doors and remove cleanly; they fail on rough or cold surfaces, so apply them on a dry day above freezing and let the adhesive cure 24 hours before hanging weight on it. For garland on frames, adhesive clips or small cup hooks on wood casing hold best; on painted trim, clips avoid holes entirely.

Whatever the hanger, wire the wreath or garland to it. A wreath hung by its own loop blows off in the first storm; a wreath wired to its hook stays put. Twist floral wire or a cable tie through the wreath frame and around the hook, and do the same at garland ends and midpoints.

## Weatherproofing: wind, rain and frost

**Wind** is the main enemy. Wire everything, weight planters, and choose faux greenery for exposed, windy porches — fresh garland sheds needles fast in wind. If your porch faces the prevailing wind, choose a swag, which catches less wind than a full round wreath.

**Rain** damages fresh greenery slowly and paper decorations instantly. Keep paper, felt and fabric decorations under cover, and bring fresh wreaths indoors during prolonged heavy rain. Faux wreaths and garlands with UV-resistant foliage handle rain without fading.

**Frost** makes glass ornaments brittle and suction cups unreliable, and it can crack terracotta planters. Use shatterproof baubles outdoors, swap terracotta for frost-proof resin planters, and use battery candles rather than real flames near greenery — a dry wreath and a naked flame are a fire risk.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Wrong wreath size.** Smaller than half the door width looks lost; bigger than three-quarters looks squeezed. Two-thirds is the rule.
- **Hanging the wreath too high.** Centre it at eye level (58–64 inches), not at the centre of the door panel.
- **Garland blocking the door.** Keep garland 2 inches outside the frame edge so the door seals and swings freely.
- **Everything decorated, nothing resting.** If wreath, garland, lanterns and planters all compete, the eye finds no focal point. Let the wreath lead and keep one element simple.
- **Glass baubles over a walkway.** They fall, they shatter, and bare feet find the pieces. Shatterproof only outdoors.
- **Real candles near greenery.** Battery candles on timers give the same glow with none of the fire risk.
- **No timer on the lights.** Lights left on all night waste electricity. An outdoor timer or dusk-to-dawn sensor costs a few dollars.

## FAQ

**What size Christmas wreath do I need for a standard front door?**
A 22–26 inch wreath for a standard 36-inch door — about two-thirds of the door's width. Hang it with its centre 58–64 inches from the floor.

**How much garland do I need to frame a front door?**
Measure up one side, across the top and down the other side, then add 1–2 feet for swag. A standard 36 × 80 inch door needs about 17 feet; two 9-foot garlands joined at the top cover it easily.

**How do I hang a Christmas wreath without damaging my door?**
Use an over-door hook (no fixings) or an outdoor-rated adhesive hook on a smooth, dry door. Wire the wreath to the hook so wind cannot lift it off.

**Can I decorate a front door if I rent or live in an apartment?**
Yes — over-door hooks, adhesive hooks and swags leave no damage, and a single wreath plus a doormat transforms an apartment door. Check building rules about hallway decorations.

**How do I keep a Christmas wreath from blowing off the door?**
Wire it to its hook with floral wire or a cable tie, rather than hanging it by its loop. For very exposed doors, choose a swag, which catches less wind.

**Should I use real or faux greenery on my front door?**
Faux suits exposed, windy porches and lasts the whole season with no shedding. Fresh greenery smells wonderful on sheltered porches, but needs replacing after a few weeks and sheds in wind.

**When should I put up Christmas front door decor?**
Most people decorate the door in late November or the first weekend of December. Fresh greenery lasts 3–4 weeks outdoors, so time a fresh wreath for early December.

Measure the door, hang the wreath at eye level, frame it with garland, and stop while the door still reads clearly. A front door decorated this way sets the tone for the whole house, before anyone steps inside.
