A wall clock is the rare piece of decor that earns its place twice: it fills a blank wall and it tells the time. Shop-bought clocks in the right size cost £60 or more; most DIY versions cost £10 to £40. This guide covers fifteen buildable clock ideas, with real costs, sizes, hanging rules and mechanism know-how.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - A mechanism kit costs £5 to £12; a 60 cm plywood face costs about £15, a resin face about £25.
> - Match the shaft to the face thickness: standard 10 mm shafts suit faces up to 6 mm; thicker faces need a long-shaft movement.
> - Size the clock to one-third to one-half of the empty wall width.
> - Hang at eye level — centre 145 to 150 cm — and anchor anything over 2 kg into a stud or wall plug.
> - Silent-sweep movements cost £2 more and are worth it in bedrooms.

## Clock Mechanisms, Sizes and Costs: What to Buy First

Every clock here starts with a £5 to £12 quartz movement — a small black box with a threaded shaft, nut and washer, and hands. Buy the movement first, then build the face around it, because the shaft length decides how thick your face can be.

There are two movement types. A **step movement** ticks once per second and costs about £5; a **silent-sweep movement** glides without a sound and costs about £7. Choose silent sweep for bedrooms and step for kitchens, where the tick is charming. 

Shaft length is the detail that ruins most first clocks. A standard shaft is about 10 mm long and suits faces up to 6 mm thick — plywood, MDF, a vinyl record, a stretched canvas. Thicker faces need a long-shaft movement (18 to 22 mm) for about £9. Measure the face first, then order the movement.

Hand weight matters too. Standard hands suit faces up to about 45 cm across. Above that, buy a high-torque movement (about £10 to £12) with longer, sturdier hands — ordinary hands droop or stall on a 60 cm face. 

| Part | What to buy | Typical cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard movement | Quartz step, 10 mm shaft | £5–£7 |
| Silent-sweep movement | Quartz sweep, 10 mm shaft | £7–£9 |
| High-torque movement | Long-shaft, faces over 45 cm | £10–£12 |
| Plywood face 60 cm | 6 mm birch ply circle | £12–£15 |
| MDF face 45 cm | 6 mm MDF circle | £6–£8 |
| **Typical first clock** | | **£12–£30** |

To assemble, find the centre with two crossed chords, then drill a 10 mm shaft hole. Push the shaft through from the back, add the washer and nut, then press the hands on — hour hand first, then minute, then second. Set the time by turning the dial on the back, never by pushing the hands round.

## Oversized Statement Clocks

Anything under 45 cm disappears on a wall wider than 2 metres, so these four ideas start at 50 cm.

### 1. Pallet-wood farmhouse clock

Five pallet planks, glued edge to edge and braced with two battens on the back, make a 70 cm square face. Sand to 120 grit, whitewash or stain, then stencil the numbers in a large serif font or use stick-on vinyl numerals. Cost: about £18 with free pallets, £25 with bought timber. Time: one afternoon. Difficulty: easy to intermediate. It weighs 4 to 5 kg, so hang it with two screws into studs.

### 2. Wire-spool clock

A cable spool end — the big wooden disc from an electrical cable reel — is a ready-made round face, usually 60 to 80 cm across, and reclamation yards sell them for £5 to £10. Sand the rim, leave the wood bare or paint it cream. Cost: £12 to £20. Time: two hours plus paint drying. Difficulty: easy.

### 3. Embroidery-hoop clock

Two wooden embroidery hoops — a 60 cm outer ring and a 45 cm inner ring — with dowels stretched between them make an open, airy clock for boho rooms. Stretch linen across the inner hoop, transfer vinyl numbers onto the fabric, and mount the movement behind. Cost: about £20. Time: two hours. Difficulty: easy, and the whole piece weighs under 1 kg, so a single adhesive hook holds it. It pairs with a [macrame wall hanging](/blog/macrame-wall-hanging/) on the same wall.

### 4. Oversized minimalist clock

A 60 cm birch-ply circle, painted matte white or soft sage, with twelve small brass dots instead of numbers and slim black hands. Mark the twelve positions at 30 degrees apart with a template, and measure twice before drilling. Cost: about £25. Time: three hours. Difficulty: easy to intermediate.

## Painted and Art Clocks

If the face is the art, the clock is a painting that tells the time.

### 5. Dot-mandala clock

A 40 cm MDF circle, painted dark charcoal or deep teal, becomes a mandala with a dotting tool set (£6) and acrylic paint in three or four colours. Work outwards in rings, letting each ring dry first so the dots stay crisp. Cost: about £15. Time: three to four hours across an evening. Difficulty: intermediate — patience matters more than skill.

### 6. Lippan art clock

Lippan is a mud-and-mirror craft from Gujarat: clay relief edged with small mirrors. The DIY version uses air-dry clay on a 40 cm MDF circle, pressed into geometric patterns, painted white, and finished with craft mirrors (£4 a pack). Cost: about £18. Time: a weekend, because the clay needs a full day to dry. Difficulty: intermediate. Seal it with two coats of clear varnish so the clay never crumbles.

### 7. Resin and gold-vein clock

A 45 cm silicone clock mould (£12) filled with epoxy resin, white pigment and a gold-vein effect made with a heat gun gives a marble look for about £30 all in. Mix the resin exactly to the packet ratio, pour in thin layers, and pop bubbles with a quick pass of a heat gun. Cost: £28 to £35. Time: two hours plus 24 hours of curing. Difficulty: intermediate — measure with kitchen scales, not by eye. Work ventilated and wear gloves; uncured resin irritates skin.

### 8. Floral watercolour clock

Paint a loose floral wreath directly onto a 35 cm stretched canvas with watercolours or diluted acrylics, leaving the centre pale for the hands. Cut a shaft hole with a craft knife and mount the movement behind. Cost: about £12. Time: two hours. Difficulty: easy — wonky petals still read as flowers. See the [pressed flower wall art](/blog/pressed-flower-wall-art/) guide for the same loose style.

## Upcycled Clocks

The cheapest clocks start as something else.

### 9. Vinyl-record clock

An old LP is a ready-made 30 cm face with a centre hole already in it. Glue a printed label or a paper design over the record, or leave the grooves showing and add stick-on numbers. The movement mounts straight through the existing hole with a standard shaft. Cost: £6 to £8 — just the mechanism and numbers. Time: 30 minutes. Difficulty: easy, and the best first clock in this guide. 

### 10. Book-page clock

A hardback book, hollowed or simply opened flat under glass, makes a literary clock for a reading nook. Glue a clock face print onto the pages and mount the movement through the book block. Cost: about £8. Time: one hour. Difficulty: easy. 

### 11. Guitar-pick and sheet-music clock

Sheet music decoupaged onto a 35 cm MDF circle, with guitar picks glued on as hour markers and a treble-clef cutout at the centre, makes a music-room clock for about £10. Cost: £10 to £12. Time: two hours plus drying. Difficulty: easy.

### 12. Moon-phase clock

A 45 cm plywood circle painted deep navy, with eight small moon-phase discs — from new moon to full moon — in place of the numbers, suits a boho or celestial bedroom. Cut the moons from white vinyl or paint them with a stencil set. Cost: about £14. Time: three hours. Difficulty: easy to intermediate.

## Small Clocks for Small Walls

These three fit kitchens, hallways and small bedrooms.

### 13. Rainbow crayon clock

Glue 12 crayons or coloured pencils radially around a 25 cm MDF circle — one per hour — with the tips pointing outwards. It takes 20 minutes, costs about £6, and doubles as a counting aid for a child's room.

### 14. Mini hoop clock

A 20 cm embroidery hoop with a circle of gingham or linen stretched inside makes a kitchen clock for about £8. Hang it on a single nail; it weighs under 300 grams. 

### 15. Chalkboard clock

Paint a 30 cm MDF circle with chalkboard paint (£8 a tin, enough for ten clocks), add white chalk numbers, and mount the movement. The numbers wipe off, so the design changes with the seasons. Cost: about £12. Time: one hour plus paint drying. Difficulty: easy. See the [chalkboard wall ideas](/blog/chalkboard-wall-ideas/) guide for the same paint at wall scale.

## How to Hang a DIY Wall Clock Safely

Weigh the finished clock on kitchen scales first — weight decides the fixing, not size.

- **Under 1 kg** (hoop clocks, vinyl records, mini hoops): one picture hook, one nail, or an adhesive hook rated for 1 kg. One fixing is enough.
- **1 to 2 kg** (MDF circles, canvas clocks, sheet-music clocks): one picture hook rated for 2 kg, or two 1 kg adhesive strips 15 cm apart.
- **2 to 5 kg** (pallet clocks, resin clocks, spool clocks): two screws into plugs or a stud. 
- **Over 5 kg**: two screws into studs, or 10 kg wall anchors. 

Hang it from a sawtooth hanger or D-ring on the back, never from the movement — it will crack under load. For clocks over 2 kg, use two fixings 20 cm apart so the clock cannot twist and hang crooked.

## Sizing and Placement Rules

The clock's diameter should be roughly one-third to one-half of the empty wall width. A 40 cm clock suits a 90 to 120 cm stretch of wall; a 70 cm clock needs 150 to 200 cm. 

Centre it at eye level — 145 to 150 cm to the centre of the face — as in the [above sofa wall decor](/blog/above-sofa-wall-decor/) guide. Over a sofa or a sideboard, leave 15 to 20 cm of clear space between the furniture and the bottom of the clock. In a bedroom, put it on the wall opposite the bed and choose a silent-sweep movement.

In a gallery wall, make the clock the largest piece, centred above a [picture ledge](/blog/picture-ledge-wall-decor/) set 25 cm below it.

## Troubleshooting: Hands, Ticking and Crooked Markers

**Hands catching on each other.** The minute hand is bent or pressed on too far down the shaft. Lift it with tweezers, straighten it, and press it back clear of the hour hand.

**Hands catching on the face.** The shaft is too short for the face thickness, so the hands sit too close to the surface. Swap to a long-shaft movement, or add a spacer washer behind the face.

**Numbers or markers look crooked.** Mark all twelve positions before gluing or painting anything, using a paper template or a protractor at 30-degree intervals. 

## Renter-Friendly Clock Ideas

Renters can hang every clock in this guide without drilling, as long as the clock weighs under 2 kg. Adhesive hooks rated for 1 kg hold the hoop, vinyl-record, mini hoop and chalkboard clocks. Command strips hold flat-backed MDF clocks up to 1.5 kg — use two, pressed onto a clean, dry wall for 30 seconds each. When moving out, pull the strip straight down, never outwards.

Heavier clocks can lean instead: a 60 cm pallet clock leans safely on a sideboard, with a rubber pad behind the top edge. The [renter-friendly wall decor](/blog/renter-friendly-wall-decor/) guide covers the same no-hole fixings, including the adhesive-prep ritual.

## FAQ

### What size wall clock should I buy or make for my living room?

One-third to one-half of the empty wall width — usually 50 to 70 cm over a sofa.

### What clock mechanism do I need for a thick wooden clock face?

A long-shaft movement (18 to 22 mm) for faces thicker than 6 mm. Standard 10 mm shafts suit faces up to 6 mm thick.

### How do I hang a heavy DIY wall clock safely?

Weigh it first. Over 2 kg needs two screws into plugs or a stud; over 5 kg needs studs or toggle bolts. Never hang it from the movement — use a D-ring or sawtooth hanger.

### How do I stop my wall clock from ticking loudly?

Swap the step movement for a silent-sweep movement, which glides without a sound. It costs about £2 more and fits the same face and hands.

### Can I make a wall clock without any power tools?

Yes — the vinyl-record, mini hoop and chalkboard clocks need no drilling. For the others, buy a pre-cut circle and the only tool you need is a screwdriver.
